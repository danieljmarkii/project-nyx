## 1. Clinical and lab artifacts vets and doctors already trust

**What the two veterinary reference labs actually ship.** IDEXX's VetConnect PLUS guide describes the result display as: "Results are color-coded by test category (such as red for hematology); an icon indicates the source of the test result"; "If other results exist for the same analyte, they are displayed in columns to the right, for easy comparison with the current result"; "Graphs on the right display analyte trends over time"; and on trending, "Six analytes are automatically graphed" with the stated rationale "Interactive trending graphs make clinically relevant changes easier for clients to understand" [EVIDENCE: S2 pdf]. The marketing page reduces to one sentence: "Compare results over time with graphical views of a patient's complete diagnostic history" [EVIDENCE: S1 fetched]. Antech's HealthTracks mobile page promises "Easy-to-read laboratory results," "visual reference intervals," "interpretative comments," and "historical trends," and the desktop product adds "Unified Trending" — "Available for overlapping chemistry and hematology test parameters across your in-clinic and reference lab results" [EVIDENCE: S3, S4 fetched]. Read: the trusted vet artifact is **a table of values with a reference interval beside each, prior results in columns to the right, and a small per-analyte trend graph** — the column-of-priors is the device vets already scan for "is this new," and the graph is positioned as a *client* explanation aid, not the clinician's primary read. Neither vendor page describes how abnormals are flagged in print; the IDEXX guide's text has no "flag / HIGH / LOW / asterisk" tokens at all (the marks live in the screenshots the extractor cannot read) — a research-debt item (§9).

**The paper flag convention.** On a paper lab report "The mark may be an asterisk, or an 'H' for high or 'L' for low or 'A' for abnormal"; electronically "abnormal labs may be display in a different color" [EVIDENCE: S12 fetched]. Schmidt's own difficulty case is the drug with a patient-specific target: he wants "TWO flags… one flag that the lab is 'above physiologic level', and a different flag if it is 'at target / normal / within range' for that specific patient," or "a third symbol / color that indicates that this lab does not have an attached 'normal range', and therefore that value should be directly inspected" [EVIDENCE: S12 fetched]. That third symbol — *no reference range attached, inspect directly* — is the honest mark for almost everything an owner logs.

**What a lab report is required to carry (the caveat floor).** 42 CFR §493.1291(c) requires on every test report: positive patient identification; "The name and address of the laboratory location where the test was performed"; the report date; the test performed; "Specimen source, when appropriate"; "The test result and, if applicable, the units of measurement or interpretation, or both"; and "Any information regarding the condition and disposition of specimens that do not meet the laboratory's criteria for acceptability." Reference intervals "must be available to the authorized person who ordered the tests," and "Information that may affect the interpretation of test results, for example test interferences, must be provided upon request" [EVIDENCE: S11 fetched]. Note the placement: the *specimen-quality* caveat is on the report itself; the *interpretation* caveats are on request. The document the vet trusts most is one that states the condition of its own sample inline and keeps the rest behind a door.

**Radiology: where the impression sits and what precedes it.** The 2005 ACR guideline lists the report's parts and their order: findings ("The report should use appropriate anatomic, pathologic, and radiologic terminology to describe the findings"), then **"Potential limitations — The report should, when appropriate, identify factors that may compromise the sensitivity and specificity of the examination,"** then **"Clinical issues — The report should address or answer any specific clinical questions. If there are factors that prevent answering of the clinical question, this should be stated explicitly,"** then comparison studies, then "4. Impression (conclusion or diagnosis) — Unless the report is brief, each report should contain an 'impression' section… A precise diagnosis should be given when possible… A differential diagnosis should be rendered when appropriate… Follow-up or additional diagnostic studies… should be suggested when appropriate" [EVIDENCE: S7 pdf]. The ESR paper's three levels of structure: "a structured format with paragraphs and subheadings… sections for clinical information, the examination protocol, radiological findings and a conclusion"; "a consistent organisation… with an internal logical order"; and "the consistent use of dedicated terminology" [EVIDENCE: S8 fetched]. Read for Nyx: the authoritative clinical document puts **limitations immediately before the answer**, and the answer is written by the clinician. An app-generated "Impression" is the one section of this grammar the report cannot borrow.

**Pathology: the element : response pair.** CAP synoptic reporting requires "All data elements and responses… in an element response pair format… (e.g., Histologic type: Invasive lobular carcinoma)"; pathologists "may choose to have two separate columns for data elements and responses (may be easier to read or preferred by clinicians)," and may add "bolding/italics or indentation to increase the readability" [EVIDENCE: S10 snippet]. The completeness evidence: "For lung cancer, overall completeness rate was 96% for synoptic as compared to 67% for narrative reports"; "Synoptic reports typically spanned more lines than narrative reports, but, at least, subjectively, the pertinent pieces of information were more easily retrievable" [EVIDENCE: S9 fetched]. The device is *more lines, less prose*: a labelled pair per line beats a paragraph for retrieval even when it is longer.

**The WSAVA Short Diet History Form — the one vet-side artifact that already models an owner's food record.** It is a single page. The table's five columns are exactly **Food · Form · *Amount · Number · Fed since**, with worked examples ("Purina Dog Chow / dry / 1 ½ cups / 2x/day / Jan 2010"; "90% lean hamburger / pan-fried / 3 oz (85 grams) / 1x/week / May 2011"; "Milk Bone medium / — / 2 / 3/day / Aug 2012"); the instruction is "Please list below the brands and product names (if applicable) and the amount of ALL foods, treats, snacks, dental hygiene product, rawhides and any other foods that your pet currently eats, including foods used to administer medications"; two footnoted measurement caveats ("*If you feed by volume, what size measuring device do you use?" / "*If you feed tinned/canned food, what size tins/cans?"); a supplements yes/no with brands and amounts; and a ruled-off block "Information below to be completed by the veterinarian: Current body weight · Ideal body weight · Current body condition score* ___/9 or ___/5 · Muscle Condition Score" [EVIDENCE: S5 pdf]. Three things transfer: the *Fed since* column (a start date beside every food — the diet-trial anchor, in the vet's own form), the amount caveat as a footnote rather than a paragraph, and a **visually separated block the clinician fills in** — the form assumes it will be written on.

**After-visit summaries — the human-medicine artifact closest to ours in provenance.** The qualitative study (39 patients, 56 clinicians): "Patients expressed a preference for the order of content items, and many wanted the reasons for medications and referrals stated… some patients were confused by multiple medication lists indicating started, stopped, and modified medications, and a single 'current' medication list was preferred by both patients and doctors" [EVIDENCE: S13 abstract]. The multi-specialty survey rated "Results of recently completed tests (91%)" and "Specific instructions on my treatment plan (89%)" most helpful, with the length complaint verbatim: "each summary from each doctor contains the lengthy synopsis… I would have preferred that this synopsis appear on the primary physician's report, rather than on each report" [EVIDENCE: S14 fetched]. Read: one current regimen list, reasons stated, boilerplate once.

**What makes these read as authoritative — the pattern across all six.** [ASSUMPTION, synthesised from S2–S14] (a) a fixed section order the reader has seen a thousand times; (b) every value beside its own reference or its own prior; (c) provenance stated on the sheet (lab address, specimen condition, "fed since"); (d) caveats *positioned*, not sprinkled — specimen caveats inline, interpretation caveats behind a door, limitations immediately before the conclusion; (e) the conclusion is the clinician's, and the document leaves room for it.

## 2. Quantified-self and consumer health reports

**Oura.** Weekly/monthly reports show "your weekly average Readiness, Sleep, and Activity scores, and trend charts for contributors including resting heart rate, HRV, total sleep time, activity goal completion"; the gates are explicit — weekly needs "at least two weeks total," monthly "at least a month's worth of nightly data," quarterly "at least 30 nights of data," yearly "more than 60 nights"; "Each quarterly and yearly report screen is sharable!"; and for professionals members "can download sleep reports (7, 30, or 90-day timeframes)… Currently, you cannot adjust the timeframes or type of data that is shared" [EVIDENCE: S17 fetched]. The redesigned app separates *daily* from *slow* metrics: "Unlike your daily metrics like Sleep Score or Daytime Stress, these metrics don't change every day," with a Vitals tab that shows "Your baseline ranges — Understand your baseline for each metric, so you know what's normal for you and when your metrics start to shift" [EVIDENCE: S18 fetched]. The 2025 Year in Review adds "four elemental personality types—Earth, Water, Fire, or Air" [EVIDENCE: S19 snippet] — the Wrapped move, and a foil for a clinical page. Transferable: **the data-minimum as a published rule**, the **personal baseline band** in place of a population norm, and a fixed-scope share-for-professionals export.

**Whoop.** The Monthly Performance Assessment is "available on the 1st of every month to any member with 14 Recoveries recorded in the previous calendar month"; its behaviour page "requires each tracked behavior to have at least 5 yes responses and 5 no responses within a 90 day period to analyze the impact"; a strain-vs-recovery page "puts all the data into context by showing how you balanced strain and recovery (top), and how your cardiovascular fitness (bottom) responded… over the past 6 months"; the sleep page compares "the current month to the previous month" [EVIDENCE: S20 snippet]. Transferable: **a numeric floor per analysis** (5 with / 5 without before a comparison renders), which is the consumer form of the report's Established-only rule.

**Apple Health.** The 2021 newsroom text: providers "can review the Apple Health app data that a user chooses to share directly in the electronic health records system"; Trends covers "20 types of data — ranging from resting heart rate to sleep to cardio fitness" and "is a new way to intelligently and proactively highlight important changes, and surfaces the information in a way that's easy to understand" [EVIDENCE: S22 fetched]. The HIG *Charts* page did not render (§ Fetch failures) — Apple's own chart grammar is research debt here, not evidence.

**Function Health.** The home "counts how many markers came back out of range" ("100 are in range and 14 are out of range"), groups by body system, and "each marker gets its own page with a plain-English explanation and a trend line"; a range "bar spans the reference range, your value is plotted as a dot, and the optimal zone is shaded separately" [EVIDENCE: S24 fetched; S26 snippet]. Two reviewers, two complaints that matter: "Did I use all of this information? Definitely not. The sheer amount of information about each marker feels overwhelming" [EVIDENCE: S24 fetched]; "A recurring theme is data overload, the sense that 100+ biomarkers without a physician to walk you through them feels overwhelming rather than empowering," against which "Function displays a small trend graph next to each marker so you can see the direction of travel at a glance" [EVIDENCE: S25 fetched]; and a text/plot mismatch — "inconsistencies where the text report and visual plot don't align" [EVIDENCE: S26 snippet].

**InsideTracker.** "your green optimized zone inside the yellow normal zone. Values outside of the normal range are in the red zones"; "the color of the data line changes to match the zone color"; rationale: "The color changes can quickly alert you to your successes as well as to biomarkers where you may want to step up your efforts" [EVIDENCE: S27 fetched]. This is the cleanest statement of the *zone-coloured line* device — and of why it is a verdict engine: the line's colour *is* the judgement.

**Natural Cycles.** The Cycle Report is "a PDF document that you can export directly from the app to share with your healthcare provider, compiling data — including symptoms and other trackers — from your last six cycles"; the app "alert[s] you if your key cycle metrics fall outside the medical norms (as defined by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, ACOG)"; and the escalation ladder: "One-time occurrences are labeled as atypical. If something is atypical for a longer period of time and becomes a pattern, then you'll get an irregular alert" [EVIDENCE: S29 fetched]. Transferable: a report named for its recipient, a fixed window (six cycles), a *named external norm* for the reference band, and a two-rung label (atypical → irregular) instead of a colour.

**Flo.** "generate a report with information about your last cycles, symptoms patterns, and most frequently logged symptoms by going to Menu – Graphs & Reports – Report for a Doctor… The report can be shared or printed… currently only available for iOS users" [EVIDENCE: S28 fetched]. Transferable: "most frequently logged symptoms" — a ranked frequency list as the summary object.

**Withings.** The Health Report PDF "can include up to 3 months of various measurements, including ECG strips, oximeter strips, and median pulse rate," and users "can select between a Standard or Compact version of the report, with the Standard option recommended as it is most used by doctors" [EVIDENCE: S31, S32 snippet]. Transferable: the vendor *offers* a compact variant and *tells the user doctors take the standard one* — the shortest evidence in this sweep that "fewer words" is not what the recipient reliably picks.

**Garmin.** Health Snapshot "records several key health metrics while you hold still for two minutes… average heart rate, stress level, and respiration rate" [EVIDENCE: S33 fetched]; per metric "you'll get a graph that shows the fluctuation throughout the recorded two minutes," savable as PDF [EVIDENCE: S34 snippet]. Transferable only as a foil: a two-minute window is a *sample*, and its report is honest because it never pretends to be a trend.

**Levels / Eight Sleep.** Levels' listing: "Track your progress with Zone Scores and daily reports"; "View lab results alongside your glucose data"; "Compare meals to see how food swaps affect blood glucose" [EVIDENCE: S35 fetched]. Eight Sleep's SleepOS post names a "New Weekly Report with sleep and health metrics" and "Design updates to Sleep Fitness Report, Health Report" without describing them [EVIDENCE: S36 fetched]; the listing describes a nightly "Sleep Fitness Score" across "the past week, month, and six months" [EVIDENCE: S37 snippet]. Nothing here on "not enough data" states — research debt.

**Chart devices used for trend + denominator + intervention, across the roster.** [ASSUMPTION, synthesised] Trend: a per-metric small line with a personal-baseline band (Oura) or a zone-coloured line (InsideTracker) or a bare direction sparkline (Function). Denominator: almost never on the chart — it lives in the *gate* (14 recoveries; 60 nights; six cycles; 5 yes / 5 no) rather than beside the number. Intervention markers: Whoop's behaviour comparison and Levels' meal comparison are *side-by-side conditionals*, not annotated timelines; none of the ten products checked documents a vertical "started X here" rule on a trend chart. "Not enough data": every product that documents it handles it by **withholding the artifact until a numeric floor is met** (Oura, Whoop, Natural Cycles' six cycles); none of the checked pages describes rendering the chart with the floor stated on it.

## 3. Financial and analytical one-pagers

**The tear sheet.** Origin: brokers "kept binders of single-page company summaries… when a client asked about a stock, the broker would tear the page out and hand it over" [EVIDENCE: S47 snippet]. Anatomy and reading budget: "designed to be skimmed in under 60 seconds"; "the first number a reader sees anchors everything else"; the sheet is "the gateway document that decides whether the data room ever opens"; six sections in order — overview, sector, drivers & KPIs, financial overview, leadership, ask — and the lead metric changes by audience ("Investment thesis + headline returns" for a fund pitch; "Quarterly performance + portfolio events" for an LP update) [EVIDENCE: S46 fetched]. The *60-second budget is the tear sheet's own design constraint* — Principle 6 has a sibling in finance.

**The fund factsheet.** "Fund factsheets are usually laid out in a standardised way so once you know what to look for, you can navigate the document for any fund"; Morningstar's one-page report carries "a high-level company or fund description, as well as key data points" [EVIDENCE: S48, S49 snippet]. Standardisation is the device: the reader's eyes already know where the performance table is.

**Stripe.** The invoice PDF carries the merchant's icon, logo and brand colour ("Brand color — Used on receipts, invoices, and the customer portal"), a memo, and a footer "often used for contractual or legally required text… such as a UK Companies House registration number"; custom fields (up to four) sit in the header [EVIDENCE: S50 fetched]. Read: the maker of the document (Stripe) is not the brand on the document (the merchant is) — the closest analogue to a report whose subject is the pet and whose author is the owner.

**Robinhood.** Bars "are colored with green by default for 'up-colored'… and red by default for 'down-colored'"; the range selector runs 1D / 1W / 1M / 3M / YTD / 1Y / All; accessibility settings offer alternative colour themes for red-green colourblindness [EVIDENCE: S51 snippet]. Foil: the line's colour is a verdict about a direction that has an agreed good side. A vomit count does not.

**Tufte's sparkline rules.** "A sparkline is a small intense, simple, word-sized graphic with typographic resolution"; "sparkline graphics can be everywhere a word or number can be"; the medical example compares a patient's glucose "against 80 prior measurements, with a gray band indicating the normal range" — "Compared to normal limits, readings above the band horizon are elevated, those below reduced"; the most recent value is accented and linked to its number; and the shape rule: "Aspect ratios should be such that time-series graphics tend toward a lumpy profile rather than a spiky profile or a flat profile" [EVIDENCE: S38 fetched]. The Powsner & Tufte "Graphical Summary of Patient Status" (Lancet 344, 6 Aug 1994, 386–389; 240 citations) is the one-page grid of small per-test plots this sweep could not read in body text; Tufte's own gloss on it is that sparklines should replace the boxes [EVIDENCE: S39, S40, S42 fetched — image-only / citation-only].

**Few's bullet graph.** Built to "replace dashboard gauges and meters" because gauges "display too little information, require too much space, and are cluttered with useless and distracting decorations"; components: a primary measure bar, a comparative reference marker, qualitative ranges, a quantitative scale; and the print-relevant rule — "The qualitative ranges are displayed as varying intensities of a single hue to make them discernible by those who are color blind and to restrict the use of colors on the dashboard to a minimum"; "may be stacked to allow comparisons of several measures at once" [EVIDENCE: S43 fetched, summarising Few 2006]. Few's dashboard definition — a single screen for at-a-glance monitoring — is snippet-grade here [EVIDENCE: S52 snippet].

**Zelazny / the McKinsey exhibit.** "The purpose of a chart is not to show data, it is to convey a message"; "Every chart and every slide should have a clear, declarative headline that states the main message"; "If you can't write the message, you don't know what you're charting"; "There are only five basic messages you can convey with data: component, item, time series, frequency, and correlation"; "The more you can remove from a chart without losing its meaning, the better"; "Your audience should be able to understand the message of your chart in less than 15 seconds" [EVIDENCE: S45 fetched]. **The grammar of the headline-row + sparkline + small-multiples page, then** [ASSUMPTION, synthesised from S38, S43, S45, S46]: a *declarative* title per exhibit (the message), a headline-number row where the first number is chosen for the audience because it anchors everything after it, a sparkline beside every number that has a history, and a grid of same-scale small multiples for the things that must be compared — with the *message* constraint being the one this record cannot always meet honestly (§7, D7).

## 4. Evidence on scannability

Ranked by how directly each bears on a clinician reading a one-page summary under time pressure.

1. **Structured summary tables halve the time to find the critical fact and double comprehension.** "including an SoF table in a review significantly improves a reader's ability to find critical information faster, reducing search time from an average of 4 minutes to just 90 seconds, and doubling comprehension accuracy compared with narrative text alone" (citing Rosenbaum et al. 2010) [EVIDENCE: S53 fetched]. The same usability study: "experts used more targeted search strategies, whereas clinicians and health care users explored a larger number of nontarget cells"; "Questions requiring comparison and integration of information across outcomes resulted in the highest rates of incorrect responses in all groups"; and, unprompted, "all 3 groups were opening and reading the 'Comments' column throughout" [EVIDENCE: S53 fetched]. Read: a table beats prose for retrieval; cross-cell integration is where every reader fails; a comments column *will* be read.

2. **Physicians prefer visual displays to tables — and a fifth find each format unacceptable.** 327 primary-care physicians: "Most (85%-89%) respondents preferred visual displays over tables… with a design with color-coded categories most preferred. However, for each format (including tables), 11% to 23% rated them as unacceptable"; "19% to 32% anticipated increased physician workload"; "Between 32% and 40% had at least some liability concerns" [EVIDENCE: S54 abstract]. Read: charts win the preference vote, but the professional reader's concerns are *workload* (will this generate a call?) and *liability* (what does the picture assert?) — both are honesty questions, not aesthetic ones.

3. **Graphs are faster than tables at the same gist accuracy; the preference is strongest at the borderline.** 106 adults: "participants took 31 seconds to view bar graphs, while those viewing tables took 40 seconds"; gist recall equal at 95%; exact values "71% giving an accurate verbatim answer across both formats"; "Preference for bar graphs was strongest when viewing tests with borderline results"; conclusion: "The current practice of presenting test results in a tabular format merits reconsideration" [EVIDENCE: S55 fetched]. (Lay readers, not clinicians — grade accordingly.)

4. **The reference display changes what the reader does next.** 1,618 adults: with a harm-anchor line labelled "many doctors are not concerned until here," perceived urgency for a near-normal ALT fell from 4.00 to 3.08 and the share wanting to contact a physician urgently from 55.8% to 34.7%; creatinine 56.7% → 35.2%; platelets — "only a mildly significant difference" [EVIDENCE: S56 fetched]. The systematic review of 18 studies: "Satisfaction and ease of use were rated higher when test results were presented using horizontal line bars with colored blocks"; "Participants who viewed near-normal results in a numerical format were significantly more likely to contact their doctor"; recommendation: "horizontal line bars with reference ranges or personalized goal ranges" [EVIDENCE: S57 fetched]. The earlier review (patient-facing visualisations): line graphs 35%, number lines 25%, bar graphs 16%, icons 12%; "bar graphs and number lines were liked and understood by most patients"; "two articles reported that line graphs were difficult for patients to interpret"; "Visualizations including a goal range produced higher levels of comprehension and decreased negative reactions to borderline laboratory results" [EVIDENCE: S58 fetched]. Counter-evidence: "accuracy is greater when a numeric value appears in tabular form" for gravity judgements, and "Laypersons were more likely to overestimate the gravity of the information when it was presented either numerically or graphically compared to the narrative format" [EVIDENCE: S61 fetched].

5. **What clinicians say they want from patient-generated data.** The scoping review (15 studies): "Users' preferences were for data to be presented with statistical summaries and clinical interpretations, alongside other non-PGHD data. Recurrent themes indicated that users desire longitudinal data display, aggregation of multiple data types on the same screen, actionability, and customization. Speed, simplicity, and availability of data for other purposes (e.g., documentation) were key to dashboard adoption. Evaluations were favorable for visualizations using common graphing or table formats, although best practices for implementation have not yet been established" [EVIDENCE: S59 abstract].

6. **How a clinician actually reads a summary — the one eye-tracking study found.** 33 nurses, three cases: "The average total gaze fixation time was 1.77 minutes… Most of this time was spent shifting between information types or making notes. 'Orders' and 'Sidebar' (mini summary of demographics and health status) were the information types that consistently emerged as key areas of focus. Participants tended to read specific information types in pairs and followed a top-to-bottom order of reading on the screen"; recommendation: "prioritizing key information types, grouping relevant information pairs, and arranging information in a top-to-bottom manner based on relevance" [EVIDENCE: S60 abstract]. A ~two-minute total, spent largely *between* things — so the pairing and the top-to-bottom order are the design.

7. **The budget in context.** Physicians "spent an average of 16 minutes and 14 seconds per encounter using EHRs, with chart review (33%)…" [EVIDENCE: S62 abstract] — roughly five minutes of chart review per human visit, of which an owner's handed-over sheet gets a slice. The tear sheet's own rule is 60 seconds [EVIDENCE: S46 fetched]; Zelazny's per-exhibit rule is 15 seconds [EVIDENCE: S45 fetched]; the shipped Nyx report's cold read put page 1 at ~4× a 60-second scan [EVIDENCE: PM note; I1 internal]. "Number needed to read" is an information-science metric — "an index of how many papers in a journal have to be read to find one of adequate clinical quality and relevance" [EVIDENCE: S64 snippet] — no study using it for clinical summaries was found among the pages checked; the phrase is a useful *design* metaphor (how many lines must a vet read before the first load-bearing fact) but not a literature.

8. **Referral letters.** A structured printed referral letter, "two letters can be printed with an A4 paper," with "Headings remind the information to be included"; users' feedback "'It saves time' 'reminds information to be included' 'looks professional'" [EVIDENCE: S15 fetched — qualitative, single practice]. Percentages circulating in search summaries (96% "easier to read," 97% preferred) could not be traced to a fetched page and are **not** carried as evidence [EVIDENCE: S16 snippet, unverified — research debt].

## 5. B&W print, photocopy and fax survival

- **The rule with a number.** "Get it right in black and white": "Black and white color schemes may be preferred in some situations where printing costs are a concern. Moreover, black and white increases the chance of those who are color blind to see and read your data visualization the same way you do"; test by "trying your data visualizations in gray scale… or comparing 2 colorized versions… and printing these out in black and white"; "Monochromatic or sequential palettes are the most resilient and suitable" [EVIDENCE: S65 fetched]. ColorBrewer marks each scheme as "suitable for photocopying" among laptop / CRT / LCD-projector / colour-print / colourblind flags [EVIDENCE: S67 fetched; S68 snippet] — and its photocopy-safe *qualitative* palette tops out at "a maximum of 3 colours! That's not often enough" [EVIDENCE: S66 fetched]. **Three is the discriminable-step ceiling for a photocopied categorical encoding** — a five-level severity scale rendered as five greys will not survive the clinic copier.
- **How to design for it.** "we have to rely only on the intensity of the colour to differentiate categories"; pattern fills make it "much easier to refer to and identify *the dotted bars* compared to the *light grey* bars"; direct labels over legends; small multiples instead of stacked categories; and the discipline — "you need to actively design them rather than assuming things will just work when printed in black and white" [EVIDENCE: S66 fetched]. Few's single-hue intensity ranges are the same rule from the dashboard side [EVIDENCE: S43 fetched].
- **How lab reports survive it.** The lab conventions are all glyphs: asterisk / H / L / A [EVIDENCE: S12 fetched]; reference intervals are text beside the value [EVIDENCE: S11 fetched]; the "column of priors" is a table, not a colour [EVIDENCE: S2 pdf]. Of the trusted artifacts in §1, every one is legible with its colour removed; of the consumer artifacts in §2, the zone-coloured line (S27) and the green/red direction line (S51) are not. No source on *fax* transmission specifically was found among the pages checked; the CLIA requirement is only that the system "ensure test results and other patient-specific data are accurately and reliably sent" [EVIDENCE: S11 fetched].

## 6. The distribution-channel angle

- **The platform's own rule for a mark on someone else's artifact.** Strava: "Strava logos can appear near, but must be completely separate and apart from (and should not appear more prominently than) the name/logo of your application"; "The font size of the Strava name should not be larger than the surrounding font, and should not appear more prominently than the name of your application"; "Never use Strava logos in any manner that implies your application was developed or sponsored by Strava"; permitted phrasings are exactly "Powered by Strava" or "Compatible with Strava" [EVIDENCE: S69 fetched]. Inverted for Nyx — where the app is the *platform* and the pet is the *subject* — the same rule reads: the maker's mark is smaller than, and separate from, the subject's identity, in a fixed phrasing.
- **The document whose brand is not its maker.** Stripe's invoice PDF carries the merchant's icon/logo/brand colour and a legally-required footer; Stripe's own presence is the rendering, not a badge [EVIDENCE: S50 fetched]; third-party checkout builders expose the Stripe badge as an opt-out ("Checked and visible by default, but you can uncheck it") [EVIDENCE: S71 snippet]. The professional recipient (an accounts department) trusts the invoice *because* it looks like the merchant's, not the processor's.
- **The share-first artifact — what to take and what to leave.** Wrapped: "Every Wrapped card is optimized for screenshots. The aspect ratio, the typography, the colour contrast, all designed knowing it will be posted"; "design the shareable artifact first, then build the experience around it"; the credibility lever is framing — "It presents 'You spent 788 hours finding yourself.' The data is identical. The framing is everything" — and the restraint — "No badges. No streaks. Just your data, beautifully presented" [EVIDENCE: S70 fetched]. Oura puts a share icon on every quarterly/yearly report screen [EVIDENCE: S17 fetched]. Take: the *artifact-first* discipline (draw the page the vet holds before the screen the owner sees) and the restraint. Leave: the reframing — on a clinical page the identical data must be *un*-reframed (788 hours is 47,283 minutes, said as minutes).
- **Reports named for their recipient.** Flo's "Report for a Doctor" [EVIDENCE: S28 fetched]; Natural Cycles' "Cycle Report… to share with your healthcare provider" [EVIDENCE: S29 fetched]; Oura's "Shareable Reports for Professionals" [EVIDENCE: S17 fetched]; Withings' "Standard… most used by doctors" [EVIDENCE: S32 snippet]. None of the four pages describes a brand treatment on the exported PDF — research debt.
- **Where the line is** [ASSUMPTION, synthesised from S69, S50, S70, I2]: a maker's mark on a clinical page reads as credible when it is (a) in a fixed, expected position (footer / colophon, where the lab's address sits on a lab report — S11), (b) set smaller than the subject line and the section heads, (c) in the page's ink, not a brand colour, (d) phrased as provenance ("Prepared from the owner's log in Culprit, <date>") rather than as an invitation, and (e) accompanied by the thing that makes a professional recommend it — the caveat discipline and the denominators — rather than by a URL. It reads as marketing the moment any of (a)–(d) fails, or the moment a paw print appears (I2, verbatim).

## 8. What a beautiful clinical one-pager is NOT

The anti-patterns this sweep saw, each with the source that shows it and the principle it breaks.

1. **Colour as verdict.** A trend line that turns green when the value is good [S27], a bar that is red when down [S51], a traffic-light zone [S57, S58]. It fails the photocopier (§5), it fails a colourblind vet, and green is a reassurance — which an n=1 owner record may not give.
2. **A reference range that does not exist.** A range bar for a count that has no population norm invents a norm and tells the vet where "normal" is [S6, S26 → D6].
3. **A harm anchor authored by the app.** "Many doctors are not concerned until here" moved 21 points of patients off the phone [S56]; on a vet report the vet *is* the harm anchor, and the app drawing one usurps the impression.
4. **The impression written by the software.** ACR's "Impression (conclusion or diagnosis)" is the radiologist's [S7]; a section under that name on an app-generated page is a diagnosis by construction.
5. **A composite score.** "Sleep Fitness Score," Readiness, Zone Score [S37, S17, S35] — one number that hides its denominators and cannot be argued with on paper.
6. **The reframe.** "788 hours finding yourself" for 47,283 minutes [S70]; elemental personality types [S19]. On a clinical page the number is the number, in the unit the vet uses.
7. **A cumulative curve that only rises.** A growth-of-10k line is the factsheet's centrepiece [S48]; a cumulative episode count draws the same shape and *looks* like worsening at any rate.
8. **Data overload dressed as thoroughness.** "The sheer amount of information about each marker feels overwhelming" [S24]; "100+ biomarkers without a physician… overwhelming rather than empowering" [S25]. This is the shipped report's own finding (~1,300 words) restated in a consumer product.
9. **Text that disagrees with its plot.** "inconsistencies where the text report and visual plot don't align" [S26]; the July cold read's 18-vs-19-days and the "5 weeks" beside 8 bars [I1] are the same defect in-house.
10. **Boilerplate on every page.** "each summary from each doctor contains the lengthy synopsis" [S14]; the July R2-4 consolidation (~23 disclaimer-class strings → ~4) [I1].
11. **The disclaimer that disappears when the page is cut down.** The "compact" that drops the specimen-condition line — CLIA keeps it on the sheet [S11]; a beautiful front page that keeps the chart and loses the coverage line is the exact trade to refuse.
12. **A nonlinear time axis that is not declared.** Recent-expanded time is a Powsner/Tufte device [S39]; undeclared, it makes the last week's cluster look like the whole window's rate.
13. **A calm aspect ratio.** Tufte's "lumpy, not spiky" is a *legibility* rule [S38]; applied to an episode series it can smooth a three-day cluster into a hillock.
14. **Charts that withhold instead of state.** Every data floor in §2 withholds the artifact [S17, S20, S29]; a report that silently omits the section has hidden the ask (D14).
15. **A brand colour on the clinical page, a logo above the subject, a URL that invites.** The Strava rule inverted [S69]; the personas' own anti-pattern — "A vet report PDF with branding, paw prints, or anything that would embarrass a vet reading it in clinic" [I2].
16. **Decoration.** Gauges "cluttered with useless and distracting decorations" [S43]; "Gridlines, unnecessary labels, and decorative elements only distract from the message" [S45]; Principle 6's "no decoration" [I2] is the industry consensus, not a house quirk.
17. **Rank without a safety pin.** A most-frequent list that lets a benign frequent item outrank a rare red flag (D21).
