## TL;DR

1. **"Reintroduction" is three different clinical events, and only one is a diagnostic test.** For skin-manifesting food allergy, feeding the previous diet again (the provocation) is "the sole means of confirming a diagnosis of food allergy" (AAHA 2023); ICADA, the CAT series and MSD agree `[E]`. For gut disease, the ACVIM 2026 consensus confirms the food-responsive phenotype from response alone; going back to the old food is "transitioning away" after at least 12 weeks, with no stated method, and dogs with protein-losing enteropathy stay on the diet `[E]`. Staying on the trial diet is a complete outcome in both, not an unfinished trial `[E]`. Human medicine keeps the jobs apart on purpose: "The OFC cannot be replaced by the milk ladder after a diagnostic elimination diet" (WAO 2023) `[E]`.

2. **Improving on the diet is not the diagnosis.** 6 of 19 dogs (32%) and 8 of 17 cats (47%) that improved on an elimination diet did not relapse when challenged (Sofou 2024; Noli 2021) `[E]`. A change of season, better flea control, a concurrent antipruritic or the diet's other properties draw the same improving curve `[E]`.

3. **A skin challenge window is 14 days, and the cat's 7 days is weaker than it looks.** CAT(9) pools 315 canine and 72 feline positive challenges: dogs TTF50 5 days and TTF90 14; cats TTF50 4 and TTF90 7 `[E]`. The cat figure rests mostly on one retrospective series binned "days 1 to 7"; in the day-resolved studies of both species, roughly one positive challenge in six flared after day 7, and all had by day 14 (§3.3) `[A on E]`. By day 2 only 21% of dogs and 29% of cats had flared, so the 48-hour to 7-day windows of human apps would miss most pet flares `[E]`.

4. **An open single-food challenge is weak evidence that the food is the allergen.** In the only placebo-controlled challenge study, 6 of 12 food-allergic dogs met relapse criteria on a placebo week; placebo positivity (18–36%) overlapped food positivity (33–42%), p = 0.5 (Sofou 2026) `[E]`. Blinded human reintroductions show the same: a glucose control "triggered" symptoms in 26% of IBS patients, beside lactose at 28% and fructose at 27% `[E]`. The whole-diet provocation is the better-supported step; ingredient-level challenges are "not always practical or necessary" (MSD) `[E]`.

5. **A flare is relative to the pet's own day 0, and fixed cut-offs miss most real ones.** The best-validated relapse definition is a more-than-doubled itch or lesion score from day 0, or the owner's own rating of moderate-to-severe deterioration. That owner rating alone caught 12 of 13 confirmed dogs (92.3%) (Sofou 2024) `[E]`. Absolute itch cut-offs would have called 25–92% of true whole-diet relapses negative `[E]`. The first itch can come within hours (61% of dogs within 12 h in one series), while a criterion-meeting relapse was recorded at 11 ± 3.8 days in another `[E]`. Elapsed time means nothing until the endpoint is named.

6. **Every source gives the same stop rule and disagrees on everything else.** A flare means stop the challenge food, go back to the trial diet until the signs settle, and only then start the next challenge `[E]`. Owner handouts disagree on the method (whole diet plus treats, a 10–20% mix-in, or single proteins first), the window (1 to 4 weeks), the time per food (1 to 4 weeks), the settling time (1–2 to 2–4 weeks), the amount and the treats `[E]`. The prescribing vet's plan is the only authority on those parameters. Outside the skin time-to-flare data, no default has evidence behind it `[A]`.

7. **The "10% re-challenge" figure is real, narrow and partly the vet's.** It is 8 of 77 owners in one Ontario survey of dogs with suspected or confirmed food allergy, filled in before a first dermatology visit (2014–15, Royal Canin-funded). Only 33% recalled their vet discussing a re-challenge, and willingness was never asked (Tiffany 2019) `[E]`. The human analog runs the same way: about a tenth of the 20,553 FoodMaestro app users who did the elimination tried even one challenge `[E]`. The reasons on record are fear of a flare, comfort, burden, never being given a plan, and "stay on it if it's working" `[E]`.

8. **Owners already reintroduce on their own, because they want off the prescription food.** Prescription hydrolysed dry food listed at about twice the price per pound of over-the-counter food for dogs and three times for cats (PetSmart, 2026-10-11) `[E]`. Owners ask "am I stuck with this forever?", test foods with windows of "a couple days", see an early "no reaction" reversed by a late flare, conclude lifelong avoidance from one exposure, and buy hair or saliva tests whose results are indistinguishable from chance, fake fur and water included `[E]`.

9. **A challenge is not an equal-risk home event.** No challenge is indicated in dogs with protein-losing enteropathy (albumin fell within 4 days of a provocation in one series), in unstable or hyporexic dogs, or in cats that stop eating `[E]`. Food anaphylaxis is rare but documented in dogs; the one expert statement found reserves gradual, possibly supervised introduction for dogs with a history of hives or swelling `[E]`. Seven of ten skin-reacting dogs had gut signs on challenge `[E]`. Nobody has measured how long a provoked flare takes to settle `[E]`.

10. **Three pet products ship a reintroduction or rechallenge function, and none meets the clinical yardstick.** PawTrial (iOS, dogs, in Pro) records owner-set windows with careful wording, "Owner-recorded observation: No observed change", but its own sample runs a 4-day challenge on days 9–13 of an 84-day trial. ThePawcess (web, dogs, $39 per dog) schedules fixed 7-day windows back to back with no settling and lets the app call the verdict: "No reaction by day 7 → safe for life. Itch returns → convicted". KibbleLab (web, dogs and cats, $12.99/mo; missed by the October brief) says "Re-feed it once to move it to Confirmed", and its sample vet PDF prints "Fish is a confirmed trigger" `[E]`. Among the products surveyed, none combines a 14-day dog window, a stated settling period, a previous-diet step first and observation-only wording `[E, among products surveyed]`.

11. **Human apps come in two kinds, and the more careful kind records a clinician's plan.** Some run the protocol themselves (Culprit: Pattern Finder enforces a 72-hour washout between tests). Others record a plan someone else wrote: "The app never proposes a trial. It records the one you were advised to run" (Digestly) `[E]`. Most put the reintroduction step behind a paywall, and AddBack's July 2026 version replaced "automatic food verdicts" with "neutral observations" `[E]`.

12. **Against today's Culprit, a vet-ordered provocation reads as owner non-adherence.** The off-diet predicate classifies a deliberately fed challenge food as off-diet, the trial card reports "28 did not", and the vet report tiles the feedings as "Feedings not matched to the trial diet". Ending the trial first hides the challenge from every trial surface, and starting a new trial row for it removes the elimination's block from the report (§12, `b901625`) `[E]`.

13. **The engine counts a vet's experiment as a finding.** Replaying the unmodified Signal engine over invented elimination, challenge and back-on-diet records: the food correlation lane named the challenge food for 105 of 288 positive itch challenges, 49 of them at the Established tier, which the vet report prints with a p-value; right after the owner goes back to the trial diet it named the trial diet's own protein in 58 of 288 vomit records; the trial-response lane kept printing "N in the trial's 70 days" across challenge days on 29 of 96 quiet gut challenges (§13) `[A on E, simulated]`. The case-crossover design "applies best if the exposure is intermittent, the effect on risk is immediate and transient, and the outcome is abrupt" (Maclure & Mittleman 2000), and a challenge is none of the three `[E]`. One elimination–challenge–re-settle cycle is below the single-case field's bar for demonstrating cause: "Examples of designs not meeting this standard include AB, ABA, and BAB designs" (WWC) `[E]`.

## §1 What follows an elimination trial: three different events

| | Skin (cutaneous adverse food reaction) | Gut (food-responsive chronic enteropathy) | Either |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The step** | A **diagnostic provocation**: the previous diet, with its treats, for up to 14 days. A flare, then a second remission back on the trial diet, confirms food allergy `[E AAHA23, ICADA-Tx, CAT9, MSD-skin]` | A **management transition**: "A diet that induces clinical remission should be fed for at least 12 weeks before attempting to transition away from the therapeutic diet" (conditional strength) `[E ACVIM26]` | **Stay on the trial diet.** "If your dog's clinical signs have resolved, you have achieved success, and your dog can remain on this diet plan long-term" (Cornell, GI) `[E Cornell-IBD]`. "Many pet owners are satisfied with improvement … and decide to continue feeding the elimination diet indefinitely" (Hill's) `[E Forrester16]` |
| **Needed to diagnose?** | Yes. "This is essential in patients that have responded well to the diet trial and is the sole means of confirming a diagnosis of food allergy" `[E AAHA23]` | No. "Response to dietary intervention … for at least 2 weeks of exclusive feeding confirms the food-responsive disease phenotype of CIE." The word "rechallenge" appears once in the consensus body, as evidence about transitioning; "provocation" not at all `[E ACVIM26]` | — |
| **Method** | The whole previous diet first, then optional single foods (§2) `[E]` | Not stated: the consensus gives no gradual-versus-abrupt guidance and no target diet `[E ACVIM26]`. The one protocol found mixed back over one week `[E Glanemann21]` | — |
| **Who should not** | Dogs suspected of immediate hypersensitivity: gradual, possibly supervised `[E Sofou26]` | Protein-losing enteropathy: "should remain on dietary management long-term to prevent relapse" `[E ACVIM26]` | Unstable or hypo/anorexic dogs; cats that are not eating (§7) `[E]` |
| **What "no relapse" means** | "Not considered to be food allergic" (Merck), subject to the dose, window, form and medication caveats in §3–§6 `[E MSD-skin]` | Not that the diet failed: the response may come "from restricting ingredients rather than immunogenic antigens" `[E Simpson23]`, or from the diet being "more consistent, lower fat, highly digestible" `[E Tufts23]` | — |
| **Relapse rates** | — | 0% to about 80% across dog studies of a return to the original diet (§9) `[E]` | — |

The two skin steps answer different questions: the provocation asks whether food matters at all; the single-food challenges ask which food `[E CAT9, MSD-skin]`. The gut transition answers a third: whether this pet can come off the diet. Human allergy practice draws the same line between a diagnostic elimination followed by a confirming reintroduction and a therapeutic elimination followed by a stepwise tolerance ladder (WAO DRACMA 2023) `[E WAO23]`. The infant home reintroduction protocol iMAP says the reintroduction should be agreed "when the trial elimination diet is first started" `[E iMAP-protocol]`.

## §2 The skin provocation, step by step

| Step | Entry | What happens | Exit | Duration | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Elimination** | Suspected food allergy; infections and fleas addressed | Exclusive novel-protein or hydrolysed diet; antipruritics allowed early | Remission: owner global assessment "good to excellent", classically ">50% improvement" | 8 weeks for more than 90% of cases; 4–6 weeks if started with a short prednisolone course | `[E CAT1, AAHA23, Sofou24, Favrot19]` |
| **Readiness** | Remission | Antipruritics withdrawn ("generally 2 weeks", longer after depot steroids or lokivetmab); day 0 recorded; a freshly opened bag of the original food (stored dry food can carry storage mites that flare a mite-allergic dog) | Still in remission off drugs | — | `[E MSD-skin, AAHA23, CAT8]` |
| **Provocation** | Readiness | "the same food items (including treats and table scraps) as before EDT for up to two weeks or until they perceived a relapse" | Flare → dechallenge. No flare by day 14 → "Food allergy is ruled OUT, consider atopy" (AAHA dog flowchart) | Up to 14 days | `[E Sofou24, AAHA23, MSD-skin]` |
| **Dechallenge** | Flare | Back to the elimination diet; an antipruritic for 1–2 weeks may be needed | Resolves within 14 days → confirmed. Does not → cytology for secondary infection; none → "does not have food allergy" | 14-day check | `[E AAHA23]` |
| **Single foods** (optional) | Confirmed diagnosis, and an owner who is willing | One food at a time, **added on top of** the elimination diet | Flare → back to the diet until settled (at least 7 symptom-free days, or owner global "good to excellent") → next food. No flare → next food | 7 days (research protocols) to 14 days ("should not be introduced any more frequently than every 2 weeks") per food | `[E MSD-skin, UV26, Sofou26, Noli21]` |
| **Maintenance** | End of the challenges, or of the dechallenge if they are skipped | Avoid the foods that flared; keep the test diet if it is balanced | — | Lifelong. Tolerance can change: "dogs can develop new allergies to foods they previously tolerated" | `[E MSD-skin, VCA-ECT]` |

**Order of the single foods.** By the pet's own diet history, "items that the patient was eating at the start of the elimination trial" (MSD) `[E]`, or by how often each food is implicated: beef 34%, dairy 17%, chicken 15% and wheat 13% of 297 dogs; beef 18%, fish 17% and chicken 5% of 78 cats (CAT(2)) `[E CAT2]`. The CAT warns that its counts "could merely reflect pet feeding habits in the preceding decades" `[E CAT2]`. Newer, smaller data sets rank differently (fish 7, wheat 6, beef 5 of 35 reactions in UV26) `[E UV26]`.

**How many foods, how long.** Dogs often react to more than one food (mean 2.4 per dog; up to 6) `[E Jeffers96, Sofou26]`; cats usually to one, though some react to five `[E ScottMiller13]`. An 8-week elimination, a 14-day provocation, a 14-day dechallenge and five foods at 14 days each, with settling after each flare, comes to about 5 to 6 months `[A]`. Ten blinded 7-day challenges took at least 10 weeks in a research setting `[E Sofou26]`. In practice most cases stop after the whole-diet step: "the majority (76/116 cats; 65.6%) of reported cats with CFA were only determined to be reactive to their commercial diets … This was also true for 91.7% of the cats in our study" `[E ScottMiller13]`.

**Shortened protocols.** A 2–3 week prednisolone course at the start lets a challenge follow "two weeks after prednisolone finished" `[E Favrot19]`. Against a confirmed diagnosis, a threshold of one relapse or fewer had 95% sensitivity with prednisolone and 63% with oclacitinib, both 100% specific (Fischer 2021) `[E Fischer21]`: an oclacitinib-shortened protocol misses about a third of food-allergic dogs `[A]`.

### 3.2 A conflict inside the repo, resolved

The July brief records CAT(9) as "dogs TTF50 5d / TTF80 7d / TTF90 14d; cats TTF50 4d / TTF80 and TTF90 both 7d". That quotes the paper exactly `[E CAT9]`. The May feeding-windows brief records "dogs median 12 hours; 98% within 7 days", cited to the same paper. **Those dog numbers are not in CAT(9)**, which puts about 9% of dogs on day 1 `[E CAT9]`. They match Shimakura & Kawano 2021: positive challenges only, owner-recorded first itch, 60.9% within 12 hours and one dog at day 10 `[E Shimakura21]`. "98%" is an inference from 45 of 46, and holds only if every other dog reacted by day 7, which no fetched source states `[A]`. The two papers time different events, the first itch an owner sees and each study's reported flare, in different populations. The May brief's own conclusion (hour-level timestamp error is negligible in a 12-hour to 14-day window) survives either way. The correction is in §V.

### 3.4 What this does to any window

- **A dog challenge judged before day 14 contradicts the data for a sizeable minority:** 18.7% of positive canine challenges in the CAT pool had not flared by day 7, and 16.2% had not by day 13 `[A on E]`. AAHA's decision rule waits 14 days `[E AAHA23]`.
- **A 72-hour quiet spell misses about half of flares.** Only 47.6% of positive canine challenges had flared by day 3 `[A on E]`.
- **Human app windows are tuned to human timing:** 48 hours plus a 72-hour washout (Culprit: Pattern Finder), 3 days plus 2–3 (Monash), 1 day plus 2–3 (Whole30), 3 or 5–7 days (AIP) `[E]`. The closest human analog is eosinophilic oesophagitis, at about 6 to 8 weeks per food, read by biopsy `[E Gonsalves12, Molina18]`.
- **Gut timing has no comparable evidence base.** One trial subset relapsed "within a week" `[E ACVIM26]`; one owner page says signs "typically appear in one to three days but can take up to four weeks" `[E VCA-ECT]`. Any gut window is a convention, not a measurement `[A]`. A gut transition may be a window rather than a date: the one protocol found mixed back over a week `[E Glanemann21]`.

## §4 What counts as a flare, and how far an open challenge can be trusted

### 4.1 Published definitions

| Definition | Used by |
|---|---|
| Owner global assessment of challenge deterioration (OGACD) moderate-to-severe, and/or a > 100% rise in CADESI-04, and/or a > 100% rise in PVAS between day 0 and the end | Sofou 2024, whole previous diet `[E]` |
| The same, plus floors (CADESI-04 > 9 and PVAS > 1.9 at the end) | Sofou 2026, single foods, double-blinded `[E]` |
| PVAS ≥ 5/10; PVAS from ≤ 2 to > 5; a PVAS rise ≥ 3 | Bizikova & Olivry 2016; Favrot 2019; Johansen 2017 (as summarised by Sofou 2026) `[E]` |
| A ≥ 2-point PVAS rise, new lesions, or vomiting or diarrhoea | Udraite Vovk 2026 `[E]` |
| Itch returns within 14 days, then resolves within 14 days back on the diet | AAHA 2023 `[E]` |
| "> 50% improvement" followed by "deterioration" | The classic bar, which Sofou 2024 calls "vague criteria" `[E]` |

PVAS (an owner-scored 0–10 itch scale with behavioural anchors) and CADESI-04 (a clinician's lesion score) are the validated canine instruments; the owner global assessments (OGATE, OGACD) are "nonvalidated" `[E COSCAD18, Sofou24]`. For cats there is no validated owner itch scale in the challenge literature; Noli 2021 used "a not yet validated double (licking and scratching) 10 cm Visual Analog Scale" `[E Noli21]`.

**Sensitivity against the compound reference (13 confirmed dogs):** the owner's global rating 92.3%, a doubled PVAS 76.9%, a doubled CADESI-04 61.5% `[E Sofou24]`. Fixed benchmarks did much worse: "the use of benchmarks (e.g., PVAS ≥5 …) or numerical increases (e.g., δPVAS >2 of 10 or ≥3 of 10) would have resulted in low sensitivity (7.7%–61.5%)" `[E Sofou24]`, and on the open whole-diet challenge they "would have resulted in negative open FCs … in 25%–91.7% of the dogs" `[E Sofou26]`. Relative criteria need a day-0 baseline `[A]`.

### 4.2 Open challenges over-call, in pets and in people

- **Sofou 2026** (12 dogs; 8 foods at 40 g a day plus 2 placebos each, up to 7 days each, owners and investigators blinded): "six of 12 (50%) dogs were positive to either the first (two of six) or the second (four of six) placebo FC". Per food 33–42%; placebo 2/11 and 4/11. "There was no difference in the probability of positive FC to at least one food item compared to placebo (Fisher's exact test; n = 12, p = 0.5)." The authors' reading: "the most reasonable explanation is the normal temporal fluctuations in the severity of AD along with the subjective evaluation of response to FCs", and the accuracy of open single-food challenges "is questionable" `[E Sofou26]`.
- **The open whole-diet challenge looked different:** owner-rated moderate-to-severe deterioration appeared in 11 of 12 open two-week whole-diet challenges `[E Sofou26]`.
- **People:** in a blinded nine-week FODMAP reintroduction, the glucose control triggered symptom recurrence in 26% of IBS patients, beside lactose 28%, fructose 27% and sorbitol 23% `[E VdH24]`. In a blinded gluten rechallenge, "Gluten-specific effects were observed in only 8% of participants", and "An order effect was observed" `[E Biesiekierski13]`. In FoodMaestro's own data, 424 users reported moderate-to-severe symptoms after a wheat-bread challenge but only 361 called it failed `[E FoodMaestro23]`.
- **Observers who cannot ask the patient:** allergists note that infant reactions "can be difficult to interpret given infants' lack of ability to verbalize", and that clinginess or fussiness "may also be present in healthy infants not experiencing an allergic reaction" `[E Bird20]`. A blinded food challenge "is commonly considered to be positive only when objective symptoms occur" `[E Bird20]`. In pets, the owner who knows what was fed is the observer, so expectation bias runs toward "reacted" `[A]`.

## §5 Dose, form and cross-reactivity

- **Dose.** In the double-blinded threshold study, 60% of reactions came at the 30 g/day step, 20% at 10 g, 14.3% at 5 g and 5.7% at 1 g; mean eliciting dose 20.8 g (median about 0.86 g/kg) `[E UV26]`. Doses rose daily, so dose and time are confounded by design, and both 1 g reactions were the same dog's day-1 pair (1 of 10 reactors, not 5.7% of dogs) `[E UV26; A]`. One teaspoon of the previous diet flared 2 of 18 dogs `[E Tinsley24]`. The proposed ideal challenge amount is "the average or the maximum daily amount of the particular food item that was consumed before the elimination diet trial" `[E Sofou26]`. Cross-contact thresholds "remain unknown" `[E UV26]`.
- **Form.** Boiled, extruded and raw forms differ in their epitopes; most positive patch reactions came with raw protein `[E Sofou26, Johansen17]`. A negative result for home-cooked chicken is not evidence about chicken kibble, and the reverse `[A]`. A flare to the original food but not to its components suggests storage mites: "perform food challenges with single home-cooked ingredients" `[E CAT8]`.
- **Cross-reactivity.** Serum IgE and in-silico work predict shared epitopes between chicken and fish, beef, lamb and milk, and across poultry `[E Martin04, Bexley19, Olivry22]`. The one clinical test, open and in 25 dogs, did not find beef–milk or soy–wheat co-reaction and concluded "each protein source should be included separately in food-provocation trials" `[E Jeffers96]`. One challenge cannot establish or exclude cross-reactivity `[A]`. Cats: "not yet determined" `[E Noli21]`.

## §6 Confounders and concurrent medication

| Rule | Source |
|---|---|
| "ideally a withdrawal period (generally 2 weeks) of antipruritic agents should be observed", longer after depot steroids or lokivetmab; it covers antipruritics generally, not only prednisolone | `[E MSD-skin]` |
| AAHA dogs: stop antipruritics at weeks 2–3, reassess at week 4. Cats: withdraw glucocorticoids at week 3 and again at week 6. Lokivetmab extends a trial "more than 60 days"; lokivetmab "should not be used in cats" | `[E AAHA23]` |
| Research washouts (Sofou 2024 inclusion): antihistamines 1 week; short-acting oral glucocorticoids, ciclosporin and oclacitinib 2 weeks; lokivetmab 6 weeks; long-acting injectable glucocorticoids 8 weeks | `[E Sofou24]` |
| Cats: drugs stopped at least 2 weeks before the final assessment | `[E Noli21]` |

**Other causes of a flare inside a challenge window:** seasonal or environmental allergy ("Environmental/seasonal changes" and "Food challenges (holidays!)" are both on AAHA's acute flare list), fleas, secondary infection, storage mites in an old bag, and dietary indiscretion `[E AAHA23, MSD-skin, CAT8, ICADA-Dx]`. Food allergy coexists with atopic dermatitis often: about a third of dogs with atopic dermatitis have a food component, and 52–57% of food-reactive dogs in one referral series had a concurrent pruritic disease, mainly atopy `[E CAT3, Loeffler06; A]`. "Nonseasonal signs with seasonal exacerbations can occur in patients with both" `[E MSD-skin]`. Long sequential challenge programs risk "major changes in the remaining factors, such as the level of exposure to environmental allergens" `[E Sofou26]`.

## §7 Safety: when a challenge is not indicated

- **Protein-losing enteropathy.** "Dogs with PLE caused by CIE or IL should remain on dietary management long-term to prevent relapse" `[E ACVIM26]`. In six Soft Coated Wheaten Terriers with PLE or PLN, serum albumin "significantly decreased … 4 days after the provocative trial" `[E Vaden00]`. 44% of dogs with inflammatory PLE never reached remission in one series, and relapse tracked "changing from the recommended diet" `[E GreenKathrani22]`.
- **Unstable or hyporexic dogs.** Diet trials are first-line only when dogs are "clinically stable and not hypo- or anorexic" `[E ACVIM26]`; extending that precondition to a challenge is `[A]`.
- **Cats that stop eating.** "Contact your veterinarian immediately if your cat refuses the food or only eats part of his meals" (CAVD) `[E CAVD-cat]`. The ISFM hospital guideline puts nutritional intervention "no later than 3 days after the cessation of eating" `[E ISFM22]`. CAVD also says the vet "may recommend switching back to your cat's previous diet for a short period to get him eating well again" `[E CAVD-cat]`: in a food log, that safety switch looks exactly like a planned challenge, so the reason is part of the record `[A]`.
- **Immediate hypersensitivity.** No anaphylaxis occurred in the challenge series (46 dogs, Shimakura; 12, Sofou 2026), but food is among the most frequent triggers of canine urticaria and anaphylaxis, and a walnut-induced anaphylaxis case is reported `[E Shimakura21, Sofou26, Rostaher17a, Rostaher17b]`. Sofou 2026 judged abrupt challenges safe only because no enrolled dog had a history of urticaria or angioedema, and wrote that "in dogs suspected of immediate hypersensitivity to food, the gradual introduction of food items, perhaps under direct medical supervision, may be indicated" `[E Sofou26]`.
- **Gut signs in skin cases.** 7 of 10 reacting dogs with skin food reactions had GI signs on challenge, one with GI signs only `[E UV26]`. Four blinded challenges were stopped on day 5 or 6 for vomiting or diarrhoea `[E Sofou26]`. Capturing only skin signs misses positive challenges `[A]`.
- **Re-resolution is not guaranteed.** In the one feline GI rechallenge series with an outcome, signs "resolved in seven cats when the test diet was reintroduced": one of eight did not `[E MandigersCat10]`.
- **Settling time is unmeasured.** The only figures are protocol rules: AAHA's 14 days, at least 7 symptom-free days, "usually 1-2 weeks" (a dermatology handout), "2 to 4 weeks" (FirstVet; Wilson & Datz) `[E AAHA23, UV26, Coyner23, FirstVet, Wilson22]`.
- **No owner handout found gives a challenge-specific "call the vet" rule or a gut severity threshold.** The nearest are general trial triggers: vomiting or diarrhoea, refusing the diet, weight loss, or no bowel movement for more than 24 hours `[E Avacta20]`.

### 9.2 Why owners stop, as the sources name it (prevalence unknown)

- **No plan, or "stay on it".** "Vet advised we try a hypoallergenic food but gave no indication of timeline or follow up"; "just leave him on the food if it's helping" `[E F1]`; "The assistant emailed me back and said we should stay on the HA if it is working" `[E F2]`. Royal Canin's owner-tips page does not mention the challenge `[E RC-tips]`.
- **The pet is better.** "many pet owners are satisfied with improvement" `[E Forrester16]`. Owners "assume it was the diet that caused the improvement … when actually it is because seasonal allergens … are much reduced" `[E Tufts17]`.
- **Fear of a flare.** "owners may hesitate to reintroduce allergens due to fear of flare-ups" `[E UV26]`. The mitigations named are clinical: "the goal is to observe mild signs like rubbing or licking, not severe symptoms" `[E Fossati-RC]`; owners "may be more likely to perform food challenge if they understand the risk is minimal and prednisolone is administered once pruritus is observed" `[E Kennis22]`.
- **Length and overload.** A two-week window "may reduce owner compliance"; consultations covering elimination and provocation "can easily overwhelm clients" `[E UV26]`.
- **The reaction is hard to read** (§4.2), and windows conflict across the sources owners meet, from "within 4 hours to 7 days" to "three to four weeks" `[E WDJ, VCA-ECT]`.
- **Human parallels:** lack of education, fear of symptoms returning, comfort in "the safety of the elimination phase", at least three appointments, no structure `[E FoodMaestro23, FODMAPFriendly, Silva25, Whole30]`.

### 9.4 The substitute owners buy

Hair and saliva "intolerance" tests sell for about $83 to $130 (5Strands "bioresonance" $83; Glacier Peak "From $129.95"), and one vendor calls its saliva test a "reliable and clinically predictive alternative to food elimination trials" `[E 5Strands, GlacierPeak, NutriScan]`. The evidence: results from allergic, non-allergic and fake dogs (fake fur, water) were "not different from that expected due to random chance", with poor test-retest reproducibility (Coyner & Schick 2019, JSAP) `[E CoynerSchick19]`; all 30 healthy dogs tested positive on at least one of three assays (Lam 2019) `[E Lam19]`; a saliva and a serum test "were not reliable" against 163 clinical rechallenges `[E UV19]`; "all other tests cannot be recommended" (CAT(4)) `[E CAT4]`. 38% of Tiffany's owners had used blood testing `[E Tiffany19]`. Tiffany's authors add that such results "may actually impede diagnosis" once owners have them `[E Tiffany19]`.

## §11 Human elimination apps and protocols

### 11.1 Two kinds of product

- **Apps that run the protocol.** Culprit: Pattern Finder: a 14-day elimination, then each test logged for 48 hours, and the next test locked until a 72-hour washout clears; ranks 2 and beyond are blurred for free users; notes "are written by AI when you allow it" (Anthropic's Claude, opt-in) `[E PatternFinder]`. FoodMaestro: a three-day ×1/×2/×3 portion ladder with a nightly "are you happy to move to Day 2?" question `[E FoodMaestro23]`. Allergy & Food Intolerance Log: an "automatic reintroduction schedule" and "Mark each food Tolerated or Reacted" `[E AFIL]`.
- **Apps that record a plan someone else wrote.** Digestly (free, 2026-10-02): "The app never proposes a trial. It records the one you were advised to run" `[E Digestly]`. Lergy: "bring foods back one at a time on your clinician's schedule and record what happened" `[E Lergy]`. FODMAP Reintro and AddBack record a clinician's or the user's own plan `[E FODMAPReintro, AddBack]`.
- **Monash sits between.** It suggests foods and per-day amounts inside a diary and lets the user set a three-state result that re-colours the food guide `[E Monash]`. A June 2026 store review says its reintroduction guide "isn't much of a guide" `[E Monash-reviews]`.
- The "11,689 users / 2,053 challengers" study the July brief credited to Monash evaluated FoodMaestro, not Monash; 11,689 was the symptomatic-at-baseline subset (§V) `[E Dimidi20, FoodMaestro23]`.

### 11.2 The shared loop

Every protocol and product runs the same machine: a readiness gate → one food against an unchanged background diet, usually with rising amounts → an observation window → stop on a reaction → back to the elimination diet "until symptoms settle" → confirm → next food → personalise → retest later `[E]`. They differ on three things: how long the window is, who judges, and what comes out `[A]`.

## §12 Against today's Culprit (`main` `b901625`)

Lane F read the product at file:line, working one example throughout: a skin dog on a 56-day duck trial whose vet orders a 14-day provocation with the previous chicken diet at the recheck.

**The schema reserves a word, not a phase.** `diet_trial_phase` (`elimination | reintroduction`) exists since migration 040; the client writes only `'elimination'` (`lib/dietTrialSetup.ts:311`, `:462`), and **no reader anywhere selects `phase`**: not the client, `generate-report`, `generate-signal` or Ask `[E]`. ## §13 The engine and the statistics of a designed challenge

Lane G read the engine at `b901625` and replayed the **unmodified** `detectSignals` evening by evening over authored elimination → challenge → back-on-diet records, under the live config and under EN-11 (off for every account). The grids are authored, so the counts mean "reachable, and not rare inside plausible parameters", never "X% of owners" `[S]`.

### 13.1 What a challenge does to each lane today

| Lane | What a 7–14-day challenge does | Simulated |
|---|---|---|
| Food correlation (case-crossover, McNemar) | Names the challenge food: the protein is in every case window because the vet put it there, and controls drift to days before or after the block. The report then prints "chicken reached the established association threshold for itching over this window (17/25 exposed cases vs 10 controls; p = 0.008)" (`render.ts:6825-6828`) | 105 of 288 itch records named the challenge food, **49 at Established**; 35 of 288 vomit records, 3 Established (38 under EN-11) |
| The same lane, just after the stop | **Names the trial diet**: carryover vomits on the first days back on the trial diet find their nearest symptom-free control on a challenge day, so the trial protein reads as case-enriched, on the evenings the protocol says to return to it | 58 of 288 vomit records (0 under EN-11) |
| The same lane, later | A challenge's flare pairs dilute every other protein's risk difference for up to 180 days (the lookback), enough to suppress a borderline later finding | Arithmetic on the shipped formula |
| Trial response (L2) and Home's standing line | Counts challenge and re-settle days as "the trial's N days"; on a quiet gut challenge it keeps printing the pre-challenge "fewer" pair | 29 of 96 quiet GI records; 111 of 288 positive |
| Joint candidates | If the trial was completed to start the challenge, a joint card's guidance flips to `feed_apart`, an instruction mid-protocol | 48 of 288 itch records |
| Safety lanes (burden, worsening, chronicity, red flags) | Fire on a provoked flare with no knowledge it was planned, which is correct; chronicity can keep a firm "book a vet visit" for 3–8 weeks after | Replayed |
| Care state (EN-9) | A provoked flare is a "tested change", so a concern already with the vet re-raises 7–14 days later, usually after the challenge ended | `[A]` |

The design is the wrong tool on its own literature's terms: the case-crossover "applies best if the exposure is intermittent, the effect on risk is immediate and transient, and the outcome is abrupt" (Maclure & Mittleman 2000) and "makes the implicit assumption that there is no trend in exposure across the referent times" (Janes, Sheppard & Lumley 2005); a challenge stopped because of the flare is event-curtailed exposure (Farrington 2009) `[E]`. Its windows are 12 h, 24 h and 72 h (`detection.ts:2715-2719`); a challenge is a sustained block with a days-long lag `[E]`.

### 13.2 What can be computed honestly

A designed challenge makes the record an A-B-A single-case withdrawal design: a settled reference (the elimination's last 14 logged days), the challenge (from the first challenge feeding), a lag tail after the last challenge feeding, and the re-settle `[A]`. What survives the house register (counts, never verdicts) `[A on E]`:

- **Per-phase counts:** dates, days, meal-logged days, sign-days and episodes. A phase under 3 days is listed but "too short to compare" ("Any phases based on fewer than three data points cannot be used to demonstrate existence or lack of an effect", WWC `[E]`).
- **The onset as a date and challenge day** ("first itch logged Jul 4, day 4"), always beside the settled count, because an early sign-day by chance is common: at a settled rate of 2 itch days in 14, a sign in the first 2 challenge days happens by chance 27% of the time, within 7 days 66%. "Time to flare" as a duration, and any "resolved", stay out.
- **The challenge food's logged days**, and any masking course or vet visit touching the window (the shipped `lib/maskingSpans.ts` rule).
- **At most one count-anchored comparative clause**, gated by the shipped exact test (`lib/rateContrast.ts`), **evaluated once** at the planned end. In 20,000 simulated null pets per scenario, testing daily from day 3 fired falsely 2.0–2.6% of the time against 0.1–1.1% for one look; with flare days clustered as real flares cluster, 10.5–13.4% daily against 3.6–7.3% once. Power is low: a dog with 2 itch days in its settled 14 needs 10 of 14 challenge days to clear the gate, and a pet that never fully settled can never clear it.
- **No probabilities.** A posterior that the challenge rate exceeds the settled rate is 0.79 for 2 sign-days in 7 against 2 in 14, data the exact test calls indistinguishable; printed, it reads "79% likely" `[A]`. Patient-facing posteriors did not move outcomes in PREEMPT or I-STOP-AFib `[E]`.

### 13.3 The asymmetry

- **Presence counts over the whole planned window plus a lag tail, from the first challenge feeding** ("intention to challenge"), so a stop caused by the flare can never erase it, and carryover signs stay in the challenge rather than making the re-settle look unsettled `[A]`.
- **Absence is described only per protocol.** A quiet window may be described only if the window is complete (at least the species TTF90 and the planned length), the challenge food was logged on at least 80% of days with no refusal logged on it, meals were logged on at least 80% of days, the settled reference was logged and quiet enough for a flare to be visible, and no masking course or visit touched it; even then, only as a sentence about the record `[A]`.
- **Why the bar is high.** Week-long open food challenges held up under blinding 57% of the time in children (95% CI 39–74%) (Venter 2007); 3% of children who tolerated a controlled challenge reacted at home the next day (Caffarelli 2001); "physicians cannot rely on lack of symptoms to make assumptions about lack of biologic disease activity" (Safroneeva 2016); owners of dogs on placebo rated improvement in 39.7% of osteoarthritis cases (Conzemius & Evans 2012) `[E]`. One A-B-A is below the WWC causal bar `[E]`.

### 13.4 Sentence shapes that stay inside the register (Lane G §4.3)

> Reintroduction of the previous kibble (chicken), planned for 14 days from Jul 1. It was logged on 8 of the 8 days before it stopped on Jul 8. Itch was logged on 6 of those 8 days, first on Jul 4 (day 4), and on 1 of the 14 days before the challenge.

> Since the challenge stopped (Jul 9–22): itch logged on 4 of 14 days, most recently Jul 12.

Forbidden near any challenge line: the shipped verdict, reassurance and causal screens (`phrasing.ts:777-802`) plus tolerat\*, pass\*, negative, positive, safe, "no reaction", "didn't react", "no flare", "nothing happened", "same as before", "no change", "not the culprit", confirm\*, diagnos\*, allerg\* `[A]`.

Lane G names 22 counterexamples a build must be red against first (its §6), among them the carryover inversion, the day-2 stop, the seasonal flare that never settles, the quiet challenge on prednisolone, the same-day feline vomit (27% of cats flare on day 1, so phase membership has to start at the first feeding instant, not at "never same-day" as `isWithinChallengeWindow` does for incidental joins), the vigilant owner, relief attrition, the multi-ingredient old diet, the Cytopoint injection at the recheck, the refused challenge food, and the report window that slides an Early card to Established.

## §14 Constraints the evidence imposes

Evidence statements about what any design runs into. Not product decisions.

1. **Skin and gut "challenges" are different objects** (§1). One flow with one vocabulary would misstate one of them, and the gut consensus does not define its transition as a test.
2. **"Stayed on the trial diet" is a complete outcome** in both, endorsed by Cornell, VCA and Hill's sources (§1). Nothing in the evidence frames it as an unfinished trial.
3. **A skin challenge window shorter than 14 days misses a measurable share of flares in both species** (§3.3–3.4); a 72-hour window misses about half.
4. **The endpoint must be named.** First itch, a ≥ 2-point PVAS rise and a doubled score are different events, hours to days apart (§4.1).
5. **Relative criteria need a day-0 baseline**, and the owner's own global rating outperformed fixed numeric cut-offs (§4.1).
6. **A single open positive single-food result is not a confirmed allergen** (§4.2), and a single quiet window is not tolerance: "lack of response … does not rule out overall food intolerance" (§8).
7. **A negative result is uninterpretable without its window, dose, form and medication context** (§5, §6).
8. **Challenges cannot overlap.** Every protocol runs one food at a time with a settling interval (§2).
9. **The prescribing vet's plan is the only authority on method, window, amount, settling and treats**, and sources disagree on every one (§8). The one universal rule is the stop rule.
10. **Some pets have no indicated challenge**: protein-losing enteropathy, unstable or hyporexic dogs, cats that are not eating (§7). Refusal of a challenge food is a health signal before it is a preference.
11. **A return to the previous diet can be a challenge, a safety switch or a slip**, and the log cannot tell them apart without the reason (§7).
12. **Skin challenges produce gut signs** in most reacting dogs (§7): capture limited to skin signs misses positives.
13. **The 10% uptake figure is not a demand figure** (§9.1): it is small, old, recall-based, industry-funded, and half its owners were never told.
14. **Owners already self-direct reintroduction with short windows and single-exposure verdicts**, and a cheaper, faster, chance-level test competes for the same question (§9.3–9.4).
15. **Handout wording cannot be reused** (§8).
16. **The tolerated list has a date.** New allergies to previously tolerated foods are documented (§2, §8).
17. **The household is the unit**: caretakers disagree on what the dog ate 44% of the time (§9.6), and undisclosed feeding is a leading contamination channel (July brief).
