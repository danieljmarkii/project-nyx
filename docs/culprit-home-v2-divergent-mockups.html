How We Feel, in one screen — and what a pet version may keep

How We Feel is a free, nonprofit emotion journal (Ben Silbermann's product team, Marc Brackett's Yale science). Its whole product is one object: the Mood Meter, a two-axis grid of energy × pleasantness with four coloured quadrants and 144 words. A check-in is quadrant → word → optional "say more"; a tap-and-hold saves the word alone; every extra step is skippable. Around that object sit a Sunday Weekly Review (gated on three check-ins, AI, opt-in, answers stored on device), a Calendar tab it moved to slot 2 this July, a Friends tab with three share levels, strategies after a check-in, and a postcard of the moment. The full sourced teardown is docs/research/2026-09-how-we-feel-teardown.md.

The finding every scientific and clinical lens reached independently: the Mood Meter measures a self-reporting subject. A pet cannot report, so an owner's placement is an observation, not a feeling, and the pleasantness axis has no honest pet equivalent — no validated owner instrument asks for it, and How We Feel's green "calm" corner is, in a cat, the early sickness-behaviour phenotype in colour. What transfers is the owner as informant: the precise observation word, the one-gesture save, structured-first-text-attached, the ritual, the cadenced review as counts, and one datum the record lacks today — the observed-absence day ("I looked; nothing unusual"), which is a denominator, never wellness.

The Look — the How We Feel variant

Organising object: the owner's observation. Home opens on one question the owner can answer in a breath — how does Mochi seem this morning, compared with her usual? — placed on a two-axis field that resolves to a precise observation word. Hold to save. "Haven't really looked yet" is an honest answer. The centre cell writes the one new datum: an observed-absence day. The record is what accumulates beneath the question.

What was refused, on the evidence: the pleasantness axis, the four colours, intensity as depth, any word that names the animal's insides (anxious, in pain), any verdict (fine, better). A low-energy placement proposes "Log lethargy?" and never writes it silently; the rating never enters a count, a floor, a trial verdict, or the coverage line — it may only raise.

The Bowl — the ritual without the grid

Organising object: the act that already happens every day. A diet trial runs ten weeks and a daily habit takes a median 66 days to form, so this is a scaffold, not a habit: the check-in is anchored to breakfast, not a clock. The trial food leads as one large target; hold means "as usual" and that is the whole floor. A second beat asks how much went and stays unrated until tapped — an untouched card never claims "finished", because intake is the disease signal. Two prefilled one-word Tells ride behind it. The evening belongs to the recap that already ships; Sunday gets a named reading.

This is what Jordan, Sam and the habit consultant each said survives the invariants: How We Feel's ritual (a set time, a one-gesture save, an optional say-more) transferred to a confirmation the app can describe, with the impression as a free rider rather than the hero.

The Day Page — the clock is the page

Organising object: time. Home is today: one vertical page ruled by the clock from 6am to midnight, the record's events at their real times, a NOW rule that moves, and no cards at all. The lane you love becomes the whole screen. Above NOW is the past's paper; below it, the day ahead, where a hollow ring marks a row the record can already describe — breakfast most days around 7:30, the evening dose — drawn as the record's habit, never as an obligation, and gone without trace when its window passes. Benign findings move below the page behind one index line; a safety card is the only card and sits above the clock.

It is the cheapest honest freshness on this page: the NOW rule and the tint move every minute, and no word is ever generated. The 200th open is a different hour of a different day and the page shows it without saying so.

The Almanac — the month as Home

Organising object: the calendar. A health record is a record of days and the wedge is a span of them, so Home is the current page of the pet's calendar: a grid of days, each cell a glyph of what that day's record holds, today's cell open beneath. How We Feel's own evidence that the calendar wants to sit beside Home is that they renamed Analyze to Calendar and moved it to tab position 2 this July. A cell holds at most one micro-mark per category, so the month can never become a heat map; a past day with no log is plain paper, identical to a future day, so the grid shows what happened and never whether you obeyed.

Your "big old empty state early in the month" is answered by structure: the grid is the trailing five weeks, not the calendar month, so it is never empty on the 3rd. The on-this-day line is a dated fact from the record ("the trial started on Aug 4"), new whenever there is one, or absent.

Chapters — the phase-rendered Home

Organising object: where in the story the pet is. A pet is always in a chapter — first week, a trial, a course, after the vet, a 48-hour watch, day to day — and Home is that chapter's page and nothing else. Flighty is the exemplar: its pre-flight, boarding, in-air and landed screens render different needs. Our phases are already in the data, and the precedence is the record's, never the owner's: a safety card is not a chapter, it is an override above every chapter; then trial › course › after the vet › watching › first week › day to day. Every chapter is composed from components the other tabs already own, so Home never grows a Home-only rendering of a fact.

This is the undrawn option C of round 3's Arrange Home brief — the record arranges the modules — taken to its conclusion. A Home that knows which phase you are in is the app that earns the daily open; a Home that changes shape is the one the owner cannot learn. The title always sits at the same height and the Today lane is always the last thing above the fold.

07

Where the table converged without being asked, and where it split

Eleven briefs were written in isolation — none saw another, and each formed its view before reading round 3. These are the places they landed together, and the three places they did not.

The valence axis does not transfer. Vet behaviourist, affective scientist, Dr. Chen, Data, colour — five lenses, five different grounds. No validated owner instrument asks for a pet's pleasantness; the green corner is the sickness-behaviour row in colour.
The owner is the informant, not the subject. Anchored "than usual" observation words, observable only, species-keyed, a closed set; "add your own" is a note. Owners are reliable on features and counts and biased on global change (the caregiver placebo effect ran ~57% in one trial).
The real prize is the observed-absence day. "I looked; nothing unusual" is a denominator the record lacks — it turns "vomit on 6 of 33 days" from a floor into a fraction, and makes the un-logged track two honest states. Never phrased as a good day.
No colour on a day, a state or a run. Four shipped families, no fifth hue; the washes fail a deuteranope; a whole-day hue is a score. Every frame on this page reads in greyscale.
The look never enters a count, a floor, a verdict, or the coverage line. It may only raise: a low-energy placement proposes "Log lethargy?"; a calm run is withheld beside an intake finding, fail-closed, the B-789 gate extended.
Sunday is counts with denominators, deterministic first. The Daily Recap's builder over seven days; a model only ever as phrasing over it, on tap, consent-gated, cached on the device. How We Feel's "because" sentence is the one thing a pet-health review may never say.
The household is a track, not a prototype. Provenance on a row, never a scoreboard; the hand-off shown by the record, never sent; a helper role that never reads the history.
The ritual reads the same on a bad morning. Habit, Jordan, Sam, Dr. Chen: the card leads, the ritual sits second and unchanged, no register shift, no haptic.

DV-2 — The write class of a look (extends DC-4)

Deciding: whether a daily look is "logging" (the FAB) or "the question Home asks" (one gesture on the first screen).

DV-3 — When the ritual happens

DV-4 — The observed-absence day as a record fact

Your questions — the ones only your household can answer

You asked what questions the team had as it ramped up. Every lens ended with a list; these are the ones that changed the work, deduplicated. The first group is for the person who uses How We Feel; the second is for you.

  1. What is on the first screen when she opens it today? The grid, a "How are you?" with today's check-ins beneath, or a Today tab with a week strip and a ring? Nobody could verify this without installing it, and it decides whether How We Feel's calm comes from one object or one question.
  2. How does she actually check in? On the reminder or when she feels something; tap-and-hold or the full walk; how many a day after the first fortnight; what the reminder says, verbatim.
  3. The nothing-day. On a day she feels nothing in particular, does she skip, log "calm", or pick a word she does not mean? That one data point is the whole quiet-day problem.
  4. The Weekly Review. Has she opened one; what did it say; did it give a "because", and did she believe it or act on it?
  5. Friends and the colours. Does she share, at which level, and has it ever felt like being watched? After a hard week, does the month read as a record or as a verdict about her?
  6. A three-morning experiment. Would she rate your pet on the How We Feel grid for three mornings and tell us which quadrant she could not choose, and whether "calm" ever felt like a guess? The vet behaviourist would trade every citation in his brief for that.
  7. For you: what drew her to it? The look, the ritual, the words, the Sunday review, the friends? The five prototypes weight those differently, and your answer reorders DV-1.
  8. Is a look an event? On the timeline, in History, on the vet report's appendix as an owner-reported observation. "Yes" is the recommended schema shape and an irreversible enum add.
  9. The write class and the hour — DV-2 and DV-3 together, because The Look and The Bowl both stand on them.
  10. Would your household log the treats on the second phone if it were two taps and nobody was keeping score? Honestly. If "no, even then", the household track is wrong and the fix is the rule card on the trial strip.
  11. The re-check date. Is it something you would enter at trial setup? It is one line on Home and the only date Jordan cares about, and it is worth nothing if nobody enters it.
  12. The next medium. Pencil on paper in her hands, or a click-through of two of these? The recommendation is paper first, because the questions above are answered by watching someone try, not by a screen.
